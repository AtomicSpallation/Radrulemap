How to read it

Each country is coloured by what its written rules and its regulator say about a private person keeping uranium or thorium minerals in a collection. The colour does not say how often anything is enforced. Solid colours mean the position is clear; stripes mean it is unclear and show which way it seems to lean.

Solid light teal, free to collect: the law or the regulator says private collections are fine, through an explicit exemption, a regulator statement, or a quantity limit so generous that normal collections stay below it.

Solid darker teal, allowed with conditions: private possession is not regulated as long as conditions are met, such as keeping specimens only as a collection (not for their radioactivity) or staying under a dose level, or there is a quantity limit high enough to keep a limited amount of high-grade material.

Solid yellow, weakly active only: keeping specimens is legal without a licence below a fixed limit on total amount or activity, but the limit is so tight that realistically only weakly active minerals fit; strongly active pieces such as uraninite need a licence.

Teal stripes, unclear but leaning free: no collector position was found, but the law has a purpose test or generous exemptions that would likely leave collections alone.

Yellow stripes, unclear but leaning weakly active only: the rules seem to allow only weakly active minerals under a tight limit, but this is not confirmed.

Purple stripes, unclear and open: only general radiation rules were found, with nothing that clearly includes or excludes mineral specimens.

Red stripes, unclear but leaning strict: the law text appears to cover specimens like any other radioactive material, but there is no collector position and practice is mixed.

Solid orange, authorisation required: a private person needs a licence or other authorisation to possess radioactive minerals above very low exemption levels.

Grey hatching, no guidance found: the country was searched but no law, guidance or regulator statement relevant to collectors was found.

A colour can change when a law changes or a regulator replies. Each country’s panel lists the sources behind its colour.

Not legal advice

Research snapshot of 1 November 2026, updated 17 November 2026 with law texts listed in the Stimson Center’s RadSecLEXIS database and on 23–28 November 2026 (country reviews and regulator replies); built from law texts, official guidance and official replies from regulators. Rules change; check with the national regulator before buying, selling, moving or disposing of specimens.

Sources

Every country lists the documents its findings come from, so each statement can be checked: law texts, official guidance, regulator reports, court or news reports, and IAEA or UN records. Where nothing was found, the entry says so. Findings based on an official reply from a regulator are marked as such; the replies themselves are not published, for privacy. If you would like to know more about a reply or have a correction or a source to add, feel free to reach out on Reddit or Instagram: atomicspallation.

Everywhere

Shipping, import and export are regulated separately from simply owning a specimen. Couriers and postal services set their own rules, and some run radiation detectors.
